Most particles are transient states, with only a few being stable particles ($e^-$, $p$, $\nu$, $\gamma$, $\dots$). Decays are governed by the following equations:
- Number of particles remaining at time $t$
$$N(t) = N(0)e^{-\Gamma t}$$
- Rate of decay assuming the nuclei only decay (they may also be created) is thus
$$\frac{dN}{dt} = -\Gamma N(0)e^{-\Gamma t} = -\Gamma N(t)$$
In nuclear physics, it is quite common to use the half-life $\tau_{1/2}$. In particle physics, we usually quote the mean life $\tau$, also called the lifetime, which are measured in the particle's rest frame:
$$N(\tau_{1/2})=N(0) /2 = N(0)e^{-\Gamma \tau_{1/2}}$$
$$\tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\Gamma}, \quad \tau= \frac{1}{\Gamma}$$
Where $\Gamma$ is known as the width of a particle's decay. Theoretically, it is given by Fermi's Golden Rule:
$$\Gamma_{i \rightarrow f} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|M_{fi}|^2 \rho(E_{f})$$
where $M_{fi} = \bra{f} V\ket{i}$ is the matrix element of the perturbation (potential) and $\rho$ is the density of final states. Particles can also often decay with more than one decay mode, in such cases the total width of the particle state is given by the sum of the partial widths (width for each decay mode):
$$\Gamma = \sum_{f} \Gamma_{f}$$
We can also define the branching ratio for a particular decay mode:
$$B_{f} = \frac{\Gamma_{f}}{\Gamma}, \quad \sum_{f} B_{f}= 1$$
