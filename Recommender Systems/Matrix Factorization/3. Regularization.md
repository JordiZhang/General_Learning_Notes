First we will look at regularization in the linear regression model then extend the idea into matrix factorization, in a similar manner. In linear regression we have $\hat{y}=w^Tx$. The idea of regularization is to prevent overfitting and allow for better generalization of the model. To do so, we add a penalizing term to the loss that penalizes larger weights. There are multiple ways of doing regularization for example L1 or L2. We will look at L2.

The new loss function under L2 regularization, also called Ridge regularization is:
$$L=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_{i}-\hat{y}_i)^2+\lambda||w||^2_2$$
Where $||w||^2_2$ is the 2-norm (Euclidean norm) squared of $w$.
It can be shown that minimizing this loss functions has the solution:
$$w=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty$$

# Extend to Matrix Factorization
We can define a new loss function and penalize large weights in the same manner:
$$L=\sum_{i,j\in\Omega}(r_{ij}-\hat{r}_{ij})^2+\lambda(||W||^2_F+||U||^2_F+||b||^2_2+||c||^2_2)$$
Where $||\cdot||_F$ is the Frobenius norm of a matrix, which is the square root of the sum of all elements squared of a matrix, essentially an extension of the 2-norm into matrices. Differentiating and solving we get:
$$\begin{aligned}
& w_i=\left(\sum_{j \in \Psi_i} u_j u_j^T+\lambda I\right)^{-1} \sum_{j \in \Psi_i}\left(r_{i j}-b_i-c_j-\mu\right) u_j \\
& u_j=\left(\sum_{i \in \Omega_j} w_i w_i^T+\lambda I\right)^{-1} \sum_{i \in \Omega_j}\left(r_{i j}-b_i-c_j-\mu\right) w_i \\
& b_i=\frac{1}{\left|\Psi_i\right|+\lambda} \sum_{j \in \Psi_i}\left(r_{i j}-w_i^T u_j-c_j-\mu\right) \\
& c_j=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega_j\right|+\lambda} \sum_{i \in \Omega_j}\left(r_{i j}-w_i^T u_j-b_i-\mu\right)
\end{aligned}$$
# Additional Information
There is also another model for matrix factorization based on a probabilistic framework. The idea is we model the matrices W and U as normally distributed matrices. Then by using maximum a posteriori estimation (MAP), we can obtain a MAP estimator which essentially looks the same as our L2 regularization solutions. This provides a statistical foundation and motivation as to why matrix factorization can be done in this way. However, we won't include it here as the mathematics are tedious and honestly unnecessary to understand this section. This also leads towards the Bayesian Approach to matrix factorization, which in essence simply means treating every variable as a random one with some probability distribution, including the prediction. It is a direct extension from the Probabilistic model. We will not cover it in the notes as it is quite advanced for simple notes as this. In fact both the probabilistic and Bayesian matrix factorizations are actual papers of their own and thus it is better to reference them directly.
