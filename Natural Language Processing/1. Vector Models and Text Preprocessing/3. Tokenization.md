Tokenization is the act of splitting string data into lists for later processing as required. There are many ways of splitting sentences up, but we will look at some of the main caveats to consider when doing so.
# Word-Based Tokenization
--- 
## Punctuation
Punctuation is clearly important to every language as it directly modifies the meanings of sentences. The sentence 'I like cats.' and 'I like cats?' have very clear distinct meanings. The question then becomes how should we tokenize? We could ignore punctuation all together and in fact this is sometimes used, but clearly we would lose information on the meaning.

One option to keep punctuation is to treat it as another token all together, so 'I like cats?' would become `['I', 'like', 'cats', '?']`.  Another approach is to instead consider the word 'cats.' and 'cats?' to be two separate tokens, `['cats.']` and `['cats?']`. The downside to this approach is that this increases dimensionality as now every word that ends a sentence has multiple variants.
## Casing
Similarly to punctuation, word meanings change depending on their casing, clearly 'danger' and 'DANGER' do not convey the same amount of meaning. Thus we would apply similar methods as with punctuation where we treat them as different tokens. Having said that, it is much more common that casing doesn't affect the meaning of a word, such as first letter capitalization at the start of sentences. In such cases it is much simpler to convert the string to lower case first and then vectorize.
## Accents
In this case accents do not alter word meanings, at the very least not in English and thus can be safely stripped. However, it is important to note that this may not be the case for all languages and thus some care is needed.

# Subword-Based Tokenization
--- 
This represents the middle ground between word-based and character-based by instead splitting up words into subwords. For example 'Walking' could be split as 'Walk' and 'ing'. We expect the vector for 'Walking' to be close to 'Walk' so we want some sort of shared representation. Thus in this framework 'ing' is a modifier token that changes the meaning of 'Walk', which matches our expectations of what a suffix does. This showcases another downside of word-based tokenization, where 'Walking' and 'Walk' are treated exactly the same as a completely unrelated pair of words such as 'Table' and 'Sky'. Using subword tokenization thus gives the model a common token through which 'Walking' and 'Walk' are related.

Although we have made a compelling argument for using subword tokenization, as all things in machine learning, they are nuanced to the problem. Not to mention that subword tokenization is not as simple as the other two, there are countless ways to split up words and deciding on rules to do so is a challenge in and of itself.
# Stopwords
--- 
Words like 'it', 'and', 'the' are incredibly common yet they convey little to no meaning. Given that they are so common, they are present in basically every document and do not actually hold any useful information on the document. It is then useful to omit these words when tokenizing/vectorizing. This also lowers the overall dimension of the vectors and therefore less computation is required, although that may only apply to the count vectorizer as other methods may not be affected other than the useless information being discarded.

The relationships between different words is represented via their differences and distance. With our count vectorizer, if we include the counts for stopwords, then the documents that are similar will simply be those with a similar count for the stopwords. These words might overshadow more important ones and thus lead to incorrect and inaccurate results. Stopwords are thus our list of words we would like to omit. This is a common procedure so it is already commonly implemented in many packages, for instance `nltk` has stopwords for many languages.
# Stemming and Lemmatization
--- 
This can be considered an extension to subword tokenization. In a similar fashion words like 'Walk', 'Walks', 'Walked' are all closely related in meaning, yet they are treated as completely unrelated words when tokenizing. This in turn leads to high dimensionality vectors but it also leads to a practical issue. Say we used our models for a search engine, then if we were to search for 'Running' we would naturally expect results related to it. However, because we treat different variations of 'Running' as completely unrelated, websites that are relevant will not match our search algorithm.

One solution is to convert words to their root word. We will look at 2 popular ways of doing so. Stemming just truncates the words at the ends, it is a crude method that often leads to fake words. Lemmatization instead is more sophisticated and uses actual language rules to return the true root of a word. The root of a word is also known as its lemma and hence lemmatization.
## Stemming
Stemming uses simple rules to truncate words. For example, words ending in 'sses' get 'es' removed, so 'Bosses' becomes 'Boss'. Of course rules like this can't account for he intricate details of language and will often give fake words as the result. Clearly, there are many ways of doing stemming and in turn many different stemming algorithms. The most popular of them is the Porter Stemmer. 
## Lemmatization
Lemmatization in practice is a complex thing and we won't be doing it manually. Instead we resort to knowledge databases to lemmatize words, these can be thought of as a dictionary that returns the lemmatized word.

| Stemming      | Better | Wa  | Mice  |
| Lemmatization | Good   | Be  | Mouse |
Words can also be both nouns and verbs depending on context and depending on which it is, they can have different lemmatizations. This is why most lemmatizers have a `pos` option, which stands for parts of speech, to control which lemmatization we want. This is not unique to nouns and verbs but also to other types of words. Therefore, to properly use a lemmatizer, we would need to first do POS tagging.
