The convolution of two functions is defined as:
$$
f(x) \ast g(x)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f(z)g(x-z)\mathop{dz}=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f(x-z)g(z)\mathop{dz}=g(x)\ast f(x)
$$
The result is also a function of $x$, so the integral has a different value depending on $x$. There are a number of ways of viewing the process of convolution. Most directly, the definition here
is a measure of overlap: the functions $f$ and $g$ are shifted relative to one another by a distance $x$,
and we integrate to find the product.
![[Convolution_of_box_signal_with_itself2.gif]]
But this is not the best way of thinking about convolution. The real significance of the operation is
that it represents a blurring of a function. Here, it may be helpful to think of $f(x)$ as a signal, and
$g(x)$ as a blurring function. As written, the integral definition of convolution instructs us to take
the signal $f(x)$ at $x$, and replace it with something proportional to $f(z)g(x-z)$: i.e. spread out over a range of $x$ around $z$. This turns a sharp feature in the signal into something fuzzy centred at the same location. This is exactly what is achieved e.g. by an out-of-focus camera.

Alternatively, we can also think of convolution as a form of averaging. Take the definition of the convolution:
$$
f(x) \ast g(x)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f(x-z)g(z)\mathop{dz}
$$
This says that we replace the value of $f(x)$ at $x$ by an average of all the values around $x$, displaced from $x$ by an amount $z$ and weighted by a function $g(z)$.

We can extend the convolution to higher dimensions as such:
$$
f(x,y)\ast g(x,y)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(u,v)g(x-u, y-v)\mathop{du} \mathop{dv}
$$
# Discrete Convolution
Replacing the integral with a summation we get the discrete convolution for functions whose domain is discrete.
$$f(n) \ast g(n)=\sum^\infty_{m=-\infty}f(m)g(n-m)$$
If $g(x)$ is a finite function over an interval $[-M,M]$, zero outside of interval, then we get:
$$
f(n) \ast g(n)=\sum^M_{m=-M}f(m)g(n-m)
$$
In multiple dimensions we extend in the same way:
$$
f(n,m) \ast g(n,m)=\sum^U_{u=-U}\sum_{v=-V}^V f(u,v)g(n-u, m-v)
$$
where $g(n,m)$ is finite when $n \in[-U,U]$ and $m \in [-V,V]$, and zero otherwise. This one is particularly important for us due to applications in image and video processing (remember the blurring interpretation).

# Cross-Correlation
The cross correlation is defined as:
$$
f(x)\star g(x)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f^*(z)g(z+x)\mathop{dz}
$$
Where $f^*(x)$ is the complex conjugate of $f(x)$. Note that the cross correlation is not commutative. In the finite discrete regime, non zero $g(n)$ for $n\in[-M,M]$:
$$
f(n)\star g(n)=\sum_{m=-M}^M f^*(m)g(m+n)
$$
If $f(x)$ is a real-valued function or has conjugate symmetry, and $g(x)$ is symmetric, then the cross-correlation is equivalent to the convolution. In fact in machine learning, the cross correlation is often used instead for efficiency purposes. The range $[-M,M]$ is only for illustration purposes. In fact we can have any finite range $[M_{1}, M_{2}]$ for the functions.
